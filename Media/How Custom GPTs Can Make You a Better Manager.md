# How Custom GPTs Can Make You a Better Manager

> Hilary Gridley, Head of Core Product at Whoop, joins *How I AI* to share how managers can use custom GPTs to scale their expertise across their team — capturing their own taste and judgment in reusable tools that deliver consistent, articulate feedback without requiring the manager to be in the room.

## The Core Idea: Getting Your Judgment Out of Your Head

One of the most valuable things a great manager can do is clearly articulate what "good" looks like. Most can't — they know it when they see it, but can't express it. The process of building a custom GPT forces that articulation, which makes it useful even before the bot exists.

Hilary's approach: reverse-engineer your own recommendation algorithm. Build a GPT that evaluates work the same way you do, then give it to your team so they can get your feedback asynchronously, at any time, on any draft.

---

## Building a GPT That Thinks Like You

### The Easy (Longer) Path — Examples First

1. Collect before/after examples of work you've edited — slides, emails, docs, anything you regularly review
2. Store them in a two-column format: original on the left, your improved version on the right
3. Export as a PDF and upload it to ChatGPT
4. Prompt: *"Here are examples of my edits. Left side bad, right side good. Can you articulate the criteria I'm using to determine what is good and bad? Only use these examples."*
5. Don't bias it upfront — let the AI interpret the examples freely first, then refine
6. Once it surfaces criteria, use: **"Be 100 times more specific"** to dial in precision
7. Quantified prompts work well throughout: "be 20% more creative", "be 1000% more specific"

### The Hard (Shorter) Path — Conversation First

- Skip examples and just chat: describe what bothers you, what you love when you see it
- The AI will derive criteria the same way, just without the examples as grounding

### In Practice

Hilary uses a hybrid — start with examples, then iterate conversationally. Once the criteria feel right, she prompts: *"Pick the most important criteria and explain your reasoning"* to converge, and *"What am I missing?"* to diverge again.

---

## From Criteria to GPT

Once you have a criteria list you're happy with:

1. Ask the AI to write the GPT prompt for you: *"My job is to create a GPT that evaluates X using my specific criteria. Your job is to write the prompt for it."*
2. Explicitly split roles (my job / YOUR job in all caps) — it helps the model stay on task
3. The prompt should instruct the GPT to: explain each criterion, explain why it matters, and give specific actionable feedback
4. Add a scoring structure — e.g. a 1–5 rating per criterion before diving into detail
5. Give it a fun name (e.g. "Deck Doctor") — makes adoption easier and signals this is meant to be used

### Why GPTs Beat Raw Prompting for Teams

- Team members don't need to know how to prompt — upload the artifact, hit enter, get feedback
- Lowers the barrier for AI-sceptical or AI-inexperienced team members
- You can create hyper-specific variants for individuals based on recurring feedback patterns (e.g. a GPT that anticipates the questions a specific exec always asks)

---

## Scaling Feedback Across a Team

- Beta test with one person — if you don't hear back, it wasn't useful enough; that's signal
- Share broadly only after you see genuine uptake
- Tailor GPTs to specific recurring feedback rather than trying to make one tool do everything
- Combine with AI meeting notes: feed in the questions that actually came up in a meeting as context for a prep or practice GPT
- The GPT handles the 0% → 60–70% lift; the manager reviews and adds the final layer — this is the leverage

## Using AI to Improve Writing (and Get Invited to Better Meetings)

Hilary's principle: *"I want it to start with me and end with me. What happens in between is between me and the robot."*

### The Workflow

1. Write a raw first draft — pour everything out unfiltered
2. Instead of asking "is this good?", ask: *"Can you succinctly express my thesis and main supporting points back to me?"* — reveals whether your argument is actually landing
3. Ask: *"What blind spots might I have?"* — surfaces gaps without triggering AI flattery
4. Ask: *"How could I make this more compelling?"*
5. Use AB testing — ask for two rewrites and compare
6. Rewrite everything yourself — AI output is a thinking aid, not the final voice; nothing goes in verbatim

### The Career Application

Hilary's advice to her team: you get pulled into important meetings, not pushed in. Write up your point of view on a strategic topic, sharpen it with AI, circulate it. Strong written ideas go viral internally. It's a way to demonstrate strategic thinking without waiting to be invited into the room.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Articulating "what good looks like" is the real output** — the GPT is just the vessel; the criteria are the value
- **Use examples, not definitions** — before/after pairs let the AI surface patterns you can't easily articulate yourself
- **Quantify your prompts** — "100 times more specific" is more useful than "be more specific"
- **AI handles 0–70%; you handle the rest** — this is the right mental model for delegation
- **Use AI to beat up your ideas, not validate them** — ask for blind spots and restatements, not praise
- **Strong writing is a career accelerant** — AI can help anyone punch above their weight on strategic communication
