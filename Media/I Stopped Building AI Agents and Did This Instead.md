# I Stopped Building AI Agents and Did This Instead

> A solo reflection from a creator and coach who spent months building AI agents, burning through API tokens, and getting poor results — until he stopped and realised the problem was his own disorganised thinking, not the tools. He introduces a three-folder system (Atlas → Projects → End Products) that mirrors the way healthy thinking actually works, and shows how the same pattern appears in Claude's interface, his Notion setup, and his Skool community structure. The core argument: your file structure is a map of your cognitive architecture, and no amount of tooling will fix a cluttered mind.

## The Mirror Principle: AI Reveals How You Think

The video opens with a confession: after going to organise his computer files, the creator found an embarrassing mess of untitled documents, folders called "new strategy," and files named "final version 3 (no this is the real final one)." The mess wasn't a technology problem — it was a thinking problem made visible.

This becomes the central insight of the video: AI externalises cognition. When you use Claude or any LLM, you are literally writing your thoughts into a system that reflects them back. The state of your folders and prompts is, in a very direct sense, the state of your mind. Most people's cognitive maps, he argues, look like a junk drawer with a search bar.

The practical consequence of disorganised thinking isn't just aesthetic — it's financial and functional. Working with poorly structured context means the AI produces poor outputs, and when using the API it means spending far more on tokens than necessary. He found he was building elaborate agents to compensate for a problem that better folder hygiene would have solved entirely.

---

## Why He Stopped Building

After hitting a wall with mounting API costs, slow outputs, and forgotten ideas, the creator made a deliberate decision to pause all building for a day. The insight that emerged during that pause was that continuing to add tools and agents on top of a disorganised foundation would only deepen the chaos.

He had been looking to second brain tools — Obsidian, Notion databases, complex dashboards — to solve what was actually a first brain problem. The realisation was simple: you cannot build a useful second brain until your first brain is reasonably ordered. Organisation has to come before automation.

This reframe is the pivot point of the video. The question shifts from "what tool do I need?" to "what is AI showing me about how I think?"

---

## The Three-Folder System

The solution he landed on came from two external sources: a paper by Jake Van Cleef arguing for radical simplicity in file structure over agent-building, and a creator named Nick (referencing "44 super agents") discussing Obsidian and second brains. Both independently pointed toward the same pattern — a three-tier folder structure.

His adaptation maps to three layers:

**Atlas** is the thinking layer. This is where raw materials live: captured ideas, half-formed strategies, notes from walks or shower thoughts. It is the inbox for cognition. Nothing here is finished; everything is in flux.

**Projects** is the building layer. This is where active work happens — content in production, plans being executed, things that have a clear trajectory toward completion. In Notion, this replaced an elaborate tangle of dashboards and databases with a single folder.

**End Products** is the output layer. Things that are done. Complete. Ready to be used or offered to others.

That's the entire system. Three folders. He now reports that his thinking falls into place naturally because on any given day he is only really doing one of those three things: thinking, building, or delivering.

---

## How Claude Mirrors This Structure

One of the more interesting observations in the video is that Claude's own product interface implicitly encodes the same three-mode structure:

- **Chat** maps to Atlas — it's where you think out loud, explore ideas, flush out concepts before they're ready to build.
- **Cowork** (Projects mode) maps to the building layer — this is where you construct artifacts, plans, and active work.
- **Claude Code** maps to End Products — this is where finished, deployable things get made.

The creator hadn't set out to find this parallel; it emerged when he looked at Claude through the lens of his new folder structure. He treats it as validation: the pattern isn't arbitrary, it reflects something real about how purposeful cognitive work is structured.

The same pattern also shows up in how his Skool communities are organised: the free Lifestyle Founders Group is the thinking/Atlas space; Claude for Coaches (paid, workshop-driven) is the Projects layer; and his VIP mastermind community is where completed frameworks and end products land.

## Beliefs That Keep People Stuck

He addresses several mental traps that prevent people from embracing simplicity:

The first is the **"better tool" fallacy** — the belief that the next app (Obsidian, Notion, a new agent framework) will solve what is fundamentally an organisational and cognitive problem. He spent time chasing second brain software before realising the answer was simpler structure, not more sophisticated tooling.

The second is the idea that **organisation is a waste of creative time**. He counters this with Bob Proctor's maxim: "Organisation is heaven's first law." In the context of AI specifically, clean folder structure means the model knows exactly where to retrieve relevant context or skills, which makes outputs faster and API costs dramatically lower.

The third is the belief that **AI does the thinking for you**. He's more nuanced here: yes, AI assists thinking. But AI also _reveals_ how you think. The quality of what you produce with AI is partly a reflection of the quality of your own cognitive organisation. People who just copy-paste outputs are missing the feedback loop.

---

## The Sophistication Trap

A recurring theme is what he calls the sophistication trap: very smart people can see so many possible ways to build or structure something that they systematically over-engineer it. The ability to perceive complexity becomes a liability when simplicity is what actually works.

He references a conversation with someone at "the school games" who pointed out how much invisible complexity goes into making something genuinely simple. Simple is not the absence of thought — it is the result of a great deal of it. The three-folder system looks obvious once you have it, but getting there required recognising that the elaborate structures being built before were solving the wrong problem.

The implication for AI use is direct: the bottleneck isn't the tool's capability, it's the clarity of the thinking you bring to it. A co-founder of OpenAI is quoted in the video as saying that the core future skill isn't coding or execution — it's _directing_. Everyone becomes a manager of AI, and the competitive advantage is the quality of your cognitive architecture.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Your file structure is a map of your cognitive architecture** — disorganised folders are a symptom of disorganised thinking, not a technology problem
- **Fix the first brain before building the second** — no tool or agent will compensate for foundational cognitive clutter
- **Three folders are enough** — Atlas (thinking), Projects (building), End Products (done); everything maps to one of these
- **AI externalises cognition** — by watching what you produce and how your files look, you can see how you actually think
- **Organisation reduces AI costs** — clean context means fewer wasted tokens and faster, better outputs when using the API
- **The sophistication trap is real** — smart people over-engineer; simplicity is a feature, not a limitation
- **The question to ask AI isn't just "what can you do for me?" but "what are you revealing about me?"**
- **Directing is the core skill** — as AI handles execution, the competitive edge shifts entirely to the clarity and quality of your thinking
